# INTRODUCTION

Humanoid robots have received increasing attention due to their human-like morphology and their potential to operate in complex environments designed for humans. Compared with wheeled or quadruped robots, humanoid robots possess a more flexible body structure, which enables them to perform whole-body dynamic motions such as walking, running, jumping, climbing, and obstacle crossing. Among these skills, jumping is one of the most challenging motions because it requires coordinated control of the whole body under highly dynamic and unstable conditions.

A complete jumping motion usually consists of several phases, including preparation, take-off, flight, landing, and recovery. During take-off, the robot must generate sufficient ground reaction force within a short time. During the flight phase, the robot has no contact with the ground and must maintain a stable body posture. During landing, the robot must absorb impact and quickly recover balance. These characteristics make humanoid jumping much more difficult than regular locomotion tasks.

Traditional control methods usually rely on accurate dynamic models, trajectory optimization, model predictive control, or manually designed phase-based controllers. Although these methods have clear physical interpretations, they often require precise modeling and careful tuning. Moreover, it is difficult to generalize them to different jumping distances, directions, and landing conditions.

Reinforcement learning provides a promising alternative for humanoid control because it can optimize complex high-dimensional policies through interaction with simulation environments. However, directly training a humanoid robot to jump from scratch is extremely difficult. The exploration space is large, valid jumping behaviors are sparse, and poorly designed rewards may lead to unstable or unnatural motions. As a result, pure reinforcement learning often suffers from slow convergence, unstable training, and low task success rate.

To address these challenges, imitation learning can be introduced to provide motion priors from human motion data or reference trajectories. Methods such as DeepMimic and BeyondMimic demonstrate that reference motion tracking can significantly improve the learning of dynamic whole-body skills. However, a policy trained only for motion tracking usually depends on full reference trajectories, which are not always available during deployment. In addition, imitating a reference motion does not necessarily guarantee task success, especially for goal-conditioned jumping tasks.

Therefore, this paper proposes a staged learning framework for command-conditioned humanoid jumping control. The main idea is to first generate diverse jumping reference trajectories through motion matching, then train a motion-tracking teacher policy using a BeyondMimic-style framework, distill the teacher into a deployable student policy conditioned only on robot state and jumping command, and finally fine-tune the student policy with reinforcement learning to improve task success and landing stability.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

1. We propose a staged training pipeline for humanoid jumping control that combines reference trajectory generation, imitation learning, policy distillation, and reinforcement learning fine-tuning.
2. We introduce motion matching to construct diverse reference trajectories for different jumping directions, distances, and landing conditions.
3. We train a motion-tracking teacher policy to transform kinematic reference motions into dynamically executable jumping behaviors.
4. We distill the reference-dependent teacher policy into a command-conditioned student policy that does not require full reference trajectories during deployment.
5. We further optimize the student policy using PPO to improve jumping accuracy, landing stability, and generalization ability.

# RELATED WORK

## A. Traditional Humanoid Jumping Control

Traditional humanoid robot control methods usually rely on model-based techniques. For jumping tasks, common approaches include center-of-mass trajectory planning, zero moment point constraints, whole-body control, contact force optimization, and model predictive control. These methods decompose the jumping motion into different phases and design control objectives for each phase.

The advantage of these methods is that they provide strong physical interpretability. For example, the take-off phase can be formulated as a force generation problem, while the landing phase can be treated as an impact absorption and balance recovery problem. However, these approaches also have several limitations. First, they require accurate robot models and contact parameters. Second, manually designing controllers for different jumping distances and directions is time-consuming. Third, highly dynamic motions often involve strong nonlinearities and contact switching, which make model-based control difficult to scale.

## B. Reinforcement Learning for Humanoid Control

Reinforcement learning has been widely used in legged robot control because it can learn complex control policies without explicitly designing full controllers. By defining appropriate reward functions, a humanoid robot can learn locomotion skills such as walking, running, turning, and recovery.

However, jumping is more challenging than regular locomotion. In a jumping task, the robot must leave the ground, maintain stability during flight, and land safely. If trained from scratch, the robot may fail to discover valid take-off behaviors. It may also learn unstable strategies, such as excessive body tilting, foot sliding, or falling after landing. Therefore, pure reinforcement learning is often inefficient for high-dynamic humanoid jumping tasks.

## C. Imitation Learning and Motion Tracking

Imitation learning provides a useful way to reduce exploration difficulty by introducing reference motions. DeepMimic and BeyondMimic-style methods train policies to track reference trajectories in simulation. These methods have shown strong performance in reproducing complex human-like motions.

For humanoid jumping, reference trajectories can provide useful motion priors, such as crouching before take-off, rapid leg extension, body posture adjustment during flight, and landing preparation. These priors help the policy learn natural and stable jumping patterns.

Nevertheless, motion tracking methods also have limitations. A teacher policy usually requires reference joint positions, reference velocities, root pose, phase information, and future motion information as input. Such information is useful during training but difficult to obtain during deployment. Therefore, the trained teacher policy cannot be directly used as the final policy.

## D. Teacher-Student Distillation

Teacher-student learning is commonly used to transfer knowledge from a privileged teacher policy to a deployable student policy. The teacher can use additional information during training, while the student only uses realistic observations available during deployment.

In this paper, the teacher policy is trained to track reference jumping trajectories, while the student policy is trained to imitate the teacher using only proprioceptive states and jumping commands. This design removes the dependency on full reference trajectories and enables command-conditioned control.

However, simple behavior cloning or distillation may still be insufficient. The student policy may imitate the teacher locally but fail to optimize global task objectives such as landing accuracy or post-landing balance. Therefore, reinforcement learning fine-tuning is further introduced after distillation.

# METHODS

## A. Overview of the Proposed Pipeline

The proposed framework consists of four main stages:

[
\text{Motion Matching}
\rightarrow
\text{Teacher Policy Learning}
\rightarrow
\text{Student Policy Distillation}
\rightarrow
\text{RL Fine-tuning}
]

First, diverse jumping reference trajectories are generated from human motion data or existing motion clips. Motion matching is used to select and stitch suitable motion segments according to the desired jumping command. Second, a teacher policy is trained to track these reference trajectories in simulation. Third, the teacher policy is distilled into a student policy that only takes robot states and jumping commands as input. Finally, the student policy is fine-tuned with PPO to improve task-level performance.

The final policy can be expressed as:

[
\pi(a_t | s_t, c_t)
]

where (s_t) is the current robot state, (c_t) is the jumping command, and (a_t) is the action output.

## B. Reference Trajectory Generation with Motion Matching

Jumping reference trajectories provide useful motion priors for policy learning. Instead of relying on a single fixed jumping motion, we construct a diverse reference dataset that covers different jumping conditions.

The motion database may contain several types of motion clips, including:

- standing preparation motions;
- forward jumping motions;
- left and right jumping motions;
- short-distance and long-distance jumps;
- take-off segments;
- flight posture adjustment segments;
- landing and recovery segments.

For each frame in the motion database, a feature vector is constructed. The feature may include root position, root velocity, joint angles, joint velocities, foot positions, center-of-mass height, short-horizon future trajectory, and phase labels such as take-off, flight, landing, and recovery.

Given a target jumping command, motion matching searches for the most suitable motion segment in the feature space. The command can be defined as:

[
c = (d, \theta, h, \psi)
]

where (d) represents jumping distance, (\theta) represents jumping direction, (h) represents target height or obstacle height, and (\psi) represents desired landing yaw.

After retrieving suitable motion segments, trajectory stitching and smoothing are applied to reduce discontinuities. The output of this stage is a reference trajectory dataset:

[
D_{ref} = {\tau_i}_{i=1}^{N}
]

where each trajectory is defined as:

[
\tau_i = {s_t^{ref}, a_t^{ref}, c_t}_{t=1}^{T}
]

This dataset is used to train the teacher policy.

## C. Teacher Policy Learning

The reference trajectory itself is only a kinematic motion and cannot be directly executed by the robot. During execution, the robot must deal with dynamic constraints, contact changes, impact forces, and external disturbances. Therefore, we train a teacher policy to convert the reference trajectory into a dynamically feasible control policy.

The teacher policy is formulated as:
$$
[
\pi_E(a_t | s_t, r_t)
]

$$
where (s_t) is the current robot state and (r_t) is the reference motion information at the current and future time steps.

The teacher policy may use privileged reference information, including:

- reference joint positions;
- reference joint velocities;
- reference root position;
- reference root orientation;
- phase label;
- current robot state;
- previous action.

The reward function for teacher training is mainly based on motion tracking:

[
R =
w_q R_q
+
w_v R_v
+
w_p R_p
+
w_o R_o
+
w_s R_s
+
w_e R_e
]

where (R_q) is the joint position tracking reward, (R_v) is the joint velocity tracking reward, (R_p) is the root position tracking reward, (R_o) is the body orientation tracking reward, (R_s) is the stability reward, and (R_e) is the energy or smoothness penalty.

For example, the joint position tracking reward can be written as:

[
R_q = \exp(-|q_t - q_t^{ref}|^2)
]

The teacher policy learns to reproduce the reference jumping motion while maintaining balance and handling contact transitions. After training, the teacher can generate stable jumping behaviors in simulation. However, because it depends on reference trajectories, it is not suitable for direct deployment.

## D. Student Policy Distillation

To obtain a deployable policy, the teacher policy is distilled into a student policy. Unlike the teacher, the student does not take full reference trajectory information as input. Instead, it only uses robot proprioceptive states and jumping commands.

The student policy is defined as:

[
\pi_S(a_t | s_t, c_t)
]

The state (s_t) may include:

- torso orientation;
- torso angular velocity;
- joint positions;
- joint velocities;
- foot contact states;
- previous action.

The jumping command (c_t) provides task-level information such as target distance, direction, height, and landing yaw.

The basic distillation loss is:

[
L_D = |a_S - a_E|^2
]

where (a_S) is the action generated by the student and (a_E) is the action generated by the teacher.

To improve stability, additional state imitation terms can be added:

[
L =
\lambda_a |a_S - a_E|^2
+
\lambda_q |q_S - q_E|^2
+
\lambda_v |\dot{q}_S - \dot{q}_E|^2
]

Through this process, the student learns the basic motion patterns of the teacher, including crouching, take-off, flight posture adjustment, landing preparation, and post-landing recovery.

## E. Reinforcement Learning Fine-tuning

Although the distilled student policy can imitate the teacher, imitation alone does not guarantee task success. For example, the student may produce motions similar to the reference but still fail to land accurately or recover balance after impact. Therefore, reinforcement learning fine-tuning is used to optimize task-level performance.

We use PPO to fine-tune the student policy. The optimization objective is:

[
\max_\theta
\mathbb{E}
[
R_{task}
+
R_{stability}
+
R_{smooth}
]
]

The reinforcement learning reward is designed around jumping performance:

[
R =
w_1 R_{distance}
+
w_2 R_{landing}
+
w_3 R_{height}
+
w_4 R_{orientation}
+
w_5 R_{stability}
+
w_6 R_{smooth}
]

The distance reward encourages the robot to land close to the target position:

[
R_{distance} =
\exp(-|p_{land} - p_{target}|^2)
]

The landing reward encourages stable foot contact and reduces foot sliding. The height reward ensures that the robot reaches the required jump height. The orientation reward penalizes large body roll, pitch, and yaw errors. The stability reward encourages the robot to remain balanced after landing. The smoothness reward penalizes excessive action changes and high-frequency oscillations.

The fine-tuning stage transforms the student policy from “being able to imitate jumping” to “being able to complete the jumping task successfully.”

# EXPERIMENTS

## A. Simulation Setup

Experiments are conducted in a physics-based simulation environment such as Isaac Gym, Isaac Lab, or MuJoCo. The humanoid robot is modeled with floating-base dynamics and actuated joints. The policy outputs joint targets or PD control targets, which are executed by a low-level controller.

The observation space of the final student policy includes proprioceptive information and task commands. The action space consists of joint-level control commands. During training, domain randomization can be applied to improve robustness, including randomization of mass, friction, joint damping, initial posture, and ground contact parameters.

## B. Basic Jumping Experiment

The first experiment evaluates whether the robot can complete a basic single-direction jump. The goal is to verify that the proposed pipeline can produce a stable jumping behavior.

Evaluation metrics include:

- take-off success rate;
- flight time;
- maximum jump height;
- landing success rate;
- fall rate;
- post-landing recovery time.

This experiment mainly tests whether the robot can complete the full jumping process, including preparation, take-off, flight, landing, and recovery.

## C. Command-conditioned Jumping Experiment

The second experiment evaluates whether the policy can generalize to different jumping commands. Different target distances, directions, heights, and landing orientations are sampled during testing.

The tested commands may include:

- short, medium, and long forward jumps;
- left and right jumps;
- diagonal jumps;
- jumps with different target landing positions;
- jumps with different initial postures.

Evaluation metrics include:

- target landing error;
- command success rate;
- landing orientation error;
- maximum body tilt angle;
- recovery time after landing.

This experiment verifies whether the policy learns a conditional jumping controller rather than memorizing a single fixed motion.

## D. Ablation Study

To analyze the contribution of each module, several ablation experiments are designed.

The compared methods include:

1. **Direct RL**
   The robot is trained from scratch using only reinforcement learning rewards.
2. **Imitation Only**
   The policy is trained only through motion tracking or behavior cloning without PPO fine-tuning.
3. **Teacher + Student**
   The student is distilled from the teacher but not fine-tuned with reinforcement learning.
4. **Teacher + Student + PPO**
   The full proposed method, including teacher training, student distillation, and PPO fine-tuning.
5. **Without Motion Matching**
   The reference dataset uses only limited jumping trajectories without motion matching augmentation.
6. **With Motion Matching**
   The reference dataset is expanded using motion matching to improve diversity.

The ablation study is expected to show that direct RL has difficulty discovering stable jumping behavior, imitation-only methods can reproduce motion but may fail in task accuracy, and PPO fine-tuning improves landing precision and robustness. Motion matching is expected to improve generalization by increasing the diversity of reference trajectories.

## E. Expected Results

The proposed method is expected to achieve better performance than direct reinforcement learning and imitation-only baselines.

Specifically, the full method should provide:

- faster training convergence;
- more natural jumping motions;
- higher take-off success rate;
- higher landing success rate;
- lower target landing error;
- better post-landing stability;
- stronger generalization to different jumping commands.

The final policy is expected to generate stable and coordinated whole-body jumping motions under different command inputs.

# CONCLUSION

This paper presents a staged learning framework for command-conditioned humanoid jumping control. The proposed method combines motion matching, imitation learning, policy distillation, and reinforcement learning fine-tuning.

First, diverse jumping reference trajectories are generated through motion matching, which provides motion priors for different jumping directions, distances, and landing conditions. Second, a BeyondMimic-style teacher policy is trained to track these reference trajectories in simulation, converting kinematic motions into dynamically executable behaviors. Third, the teacher policy is distilled into a student policy that only depends on robot proprioceptive states and jumping commands. Finally, PPO fine-tuning is applied to improve task success rate, landing accuracy, and post-landing stability.

The core idea of this work is to use reference motion data to answer “how to jump,” imitation learning to make the robot “able to jump,” and reinforcement learning to make the robot “jump well.” Compared with direct reinforcement learning from scratch, the proposed framework reduces exploration difficulty and improves the stability and naturalness of learned jumping behaviors.

Future work will focus on expanding the reference motion dataset, improving motion matching quality, increasing the range of jumping commands, and validating the learned policy in more challenging simulation and real-world transfer scenarios. In addition, robustness to external disturbances, uneven terrain, and consecutive jumping tasks will be further investigated.
